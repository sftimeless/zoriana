ZORIANA · DATA & METHOD

Real observations.
Clear boundaries.

The lunar mosaic comes from NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. Sun and Earth positions are requested from NASA/JPL Horizons for your coordinates and dates.

Visibility
Body center above the sampled local terrain horizon. Craters, ridges and lunar curvature affect visibility. Eclipses, antenna constraints, solar-array geometry and usable power are not modeled. Terrain off compares the same ephemerides against a flat reference horizon.
Libration & orbit
Horizons computes each position for an observer fixed to the lunar surface at your coordinates, so the Moon’s libration and orbital variations are already included in the Sun and Earth angles shown here. No atmospheric refraction is applied because the Moon has no atmosphere.
Sampling
Hourly JPL positions for the 30 days after the landing date, interpolated against the terrain horizon every five minutes. Durations, countdowns and crossings are estimates; short events and unresolved obstacles can be missed. Mission totals begin at 00:00 UTC on the selected start date.
Lunar day & comparison
The lunar-day chart covers 30 days, slightly more than one 29.5-day cycle of lunar day and night. Its bars score each possible landing day by the sunlit hours of the selected stay; “best” means most sunlit hours, with Earth visibility as the tie-break, and is not a mission recommendation. A longest stretch marked “≥” touches the edge of the 30-day window and may continue beyond it. Comparison options are a site on a landing date, aligned by mission time; differences are shown against the first option.
Terrain & location
LOLA elevation grids. Planetocentric latitude, east-positive longitude, a 1,737.4 km reference radius and an observer 2 m above the sampled surface. Select a site for its data resolution. Named pins are exploration points, not certified landing sites.
Map
The gold and blue lines from a selected site point toward the Sun and Earth at the chosen time. They are drawn dashed when the body is hidden by terrain or below the horizon. Their length is illustrative. Two dashed markers show where the Sun and Earth are directly overhead; the Earth marker wanders around the centre of the near side as the Moon librates. Earth itself is drawn in its true direction at its true size and mean distance, lit by the same Sun, using NASA Blue Marble imagery (December, cloud-free) turned to show the face that points at the Moon at the chosen time. That turn comes from Earth’s rotation angle and the IAU model of the Moon’s rotation, which matches the JPL Sun directions used here to within about 0.15°. Its night side is drawn faintly so the globe stays readable when it is a thin crescent; clouds, city lights and seasonal changes are not shown. In Satellite mode, or whenever the Sunlight layer is off, Earth shows the plain imagery with no lighting, just as the Moon does. The Sun is drawn in its true direction at its true apparent size but far closer than it really is. Both come from the same JPL positions as everything else; Earth’s is shifted from the site to the Moon’s centre using its mean distance.
Landing view
A tilted camera orbits the selected site over a true-scale terrain mesh (no vertical exaggeration) built from the same averaged LOLA grids as the shadow layer: 160 m spacing south of 86.6°S within about 48 km of the site, 3.8 km elsewhere within about 260 km. Drag to change heading and tilt; the compass shows north. A faint camera-side fill keeps relief readable in shadow and is not sunlight. The view is illustrative; site metrics use the finer horizon model.
Rendering
8K NASA CGI Moon Kit mosaic with a downsampled LOLA relief texture. Full elevation grids are processed separately for site visibility. The Sunlight layer traces rays toward the JPL Sun through LOLA elevations on a curved Moon. Ridges and crater walls cast moving shadows. For responsive interaction, visual elevations are averaged to 160 m south of 85°S and 3.8 km elsewhere; shadow masks refine after time scrubbing stops (about 300 m polar pixels and 5.3 km equatorial pixels). Sparse ray steps up to 500 km, height bias and map sampling can miss small obstacles or narrow shadows. This is approximate visual coverage, separate from the finer site horizon calculations. The mosaic contains some baked lighting which cannot move. Satellite defaults to unlit imagery; use the Sun button to show sunlight in any mode.
